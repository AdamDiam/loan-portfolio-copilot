## Answers

**r01. Which credit institutions must set up a dedicated NPE strategy under the EBA guidelines?**

Credit institutions with gross NPL ratios at a level of “5% or above” should establish an NPE strategy, as part of their overall strategy, and related governance and operational arrangements. [3] The guidelines are addressed to both competent authorities and credit institutions, and it is for credit institutions to set out NPE strategies, where required. [5]

**r02. How long is the probation period before a performing forborne exposure can stop being classified as forborne?**

The probation period is a “minimum of two years” since the later of the date of the concession or the date of reclassification from non-performing. [4] The elapsing of the two-year period does not automatically lead to removal of the identification of the exposure as “forborne”; the borrower must also meet the other conditions, including regular payments and no other transactions more than 30 days past due. [1][4]

**r03. When can a defaulted exposure return to non-defaulted status?**

A defaulted exposure can return to non-defaulted status only after the applicable minimum period under Article 178(5) has passed and the reclassification conditions are met. [1][4] As a general rule, institutions should consider that no trigger of default continues to apply where at least “3 months” have passed since the moment the conditions referred to in paragraph 71 are met; however, where “distressed restructuring” applies, they should consider that no trigger of default continues to apply only where at least “1 year” has passed from the latest of the specified events. [1][4] After that one-year period, the exposure should be reclassified to non-defaulted status if the conditions in paragraph 73 are met, including that “a material payment has been made by the obligor.” [4] Institutions may specify longer periods for exposures classified as defaulted in the preceding “24 months.” [2]

**r04. What is the minimum period before an exposure under distressed restructuring can be reclassified as non-defaulted?**

The minimum period is “at least 1 year” from the latest of: “(a) the moment of extending the restructuring measures; (b) the moment when the exposure has been classified as defaulted; (c) the end of the grace period included in the restructuring arrangements.” [1] Institutions should reclassify the exposure to a non-defaulted status only after that “one year period” and when the other conditions in paragraph 73 are met. [1]

**r05. What is a distressed restructuring according to the definition of default guidelines?**

A distressed restructuring should be considered to have occurred when concessions have been extended towards a debtor facing or about to face difficulties in meeting its financial commitments. [3] For forborne exposures, the obligor should be classified as defaulted only where the relevant forbearance measures are likely to result in a diminished financial obligation. [3] Institutions should set a threshold for the diminished financial obligation; if the diminished financial obligation is below that threshold, they should assess other indications of unlikeliness to pay. [3][1] Any concession extended to an obligor already in default should lead to classify the obligor as a distressed restructuring. [1][2]

**r06. Which indications of unlikeliness to pay can be based on external information?**

Indications of unlikeliness to pay that can be based on external information include: “significant delays in payments to other creditors” recorded in the relevant credit register, and “a crisis of the sector in which the counterparty operates combined with a weak position of the counterparty in this sector.” [5] Banks should also take into account external databases, including “credit registers, macroeconomic indicators and public information sources, including press articles and financial analyst’s reports.” [5]

**r07. How often should the collateral of non-performing exposures be revalued?**

Collateral of non-performing exposures should be updated “at the time when the exposure is classified as non-performing” and “at least annually while it continues to be classified as such.” [1][3] For immovable property collateral, banks should also update individual valuations “at a minimum every year for commercial immovable property and every three years for residential immoveable property.” [3] For collateral subject to indexation or similar methods, the indexation should be updated “at least annually,” and for properties with an updated individual valuation within the past “12 months,” the value may be indexed up to the impairment review period. [1][3] The EBA confirms that this frequency applies to both immovable and movable properties. [4]

**r08. Can indexed valuations be used instead of individual valuations for NPL collateral?**

Yes, but only in a limited case: valuations derived from indexation or any other automated processes are “indexed valuations” and “do not constitute a revaluation or an individual property valuation.” [2] They “may be used to update the valuation for non-performing loans of less than 300,000 euro in gross value, which are secured by immovable property collateral” if “the collateral to be valued is susceptible to measurement by such methods.” [2] This exception does not remove the “minimum requirements of Article 208(3) of the CRR,” and it does not override any more conservative national threshold for individual valuations. [2] For NPEs more generally, the collateral value should be updated on an individual basis when the loan is classified as non-performing and at least annually while it remains non-performing, with indexation allowed only under the specific threshold exception. [4]

**r09. What is the maximum time horizon for short-term forbearance measures?**

Short-term forbearance measures should generally not exceed “two years” and, for “project finance and the construction of commercial property,” they should not exceed “one year.” [1][3] [2] also states that short-term measures are applied temporarily, with the borrower able to repay from the expiry date of the short-term temporary arrangement.

**r10. What are the two criteria that make an exposure non-performing?**

An exposure is non-performing if it meets either of these two criteria: (a) it is a material exposure that is “more than 90 days past-due”; or (b) the debtor is assessed as “unlikely to pay its credit obligations in full without realisation of collateral,” regardless of whether there is any past-due amount or how many days past due it is. [6]

**r11. What signs indicate that a borrower is in financial difficulty when a loan is modified?**

When assessing financial difficulties for a loan modification or refinancing, credit institutions should consider at least these rebuttable circumstances: the borrower/facility being “more than 30 days past due during the three months prior to its modification or refinancing,” an “increase in probability of default (PD) of credit institution’s internal rating class during the three months prior to its modification or refinancing,” and “presence on a watch list during the three months prior to its modification or refinancing.” [3][5] The assessment should be based on the borrower’s situation only, “disregarding collateral or any guarantees provided by third parties.” [1][2] The guidance also says that financial difficulties may exist even where the borrower has no apparent difficulties, if “market conditions have changed significantly in a way that could impact the borrower’s ability to repay,” for example for “bullet loans” or “foreign currency loans.” [1][2]

**r12. Which groups of exposures deserve particular attention when deciding on NPE write-offs?**

Credit institutions should pay particular attention to exposures with prolonged arrears, because different thresholds may be appropriate for different portfolios and recoverability should be assessed when the borrower has been in arrears for a prolonged period of time. [1][2] They should also pay particular attention to exposures under an insolvency procedure, especially where collateralisation is low and estimated recoveries are expected to be very low because legal expenses may absorb a significant portion of bankruptcy proceeds. [1][2] In addition, a partial write-off may be justified where there is evidence that the borrower is unable to repay the exposure in full, meaning there is a reasonable expectation of recovering only part of the exposure. [1][2]
